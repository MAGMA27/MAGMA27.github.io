## 1. A conversation that begins with “learn to be a plumber”

The interview opens with career advice. The host asks how young people should plan their careers in a world where superintelligence already exists. Hinton's answer is simple: learn to be a plumber. The answer returns at the end of the show, this time with his reasoning: it will take a long time for machines to catch up with humans at physical tasks, so plumbing is “a pretty good bet”—at least until humanoid robots arrive.

This is worth putting up front because it is not just a rhetorical flourish. Over the next eighty-odd minutes, Hinton returns to the same judgment: digital intelligence is structurally better suited to accumulating and transmitting knowledge, and human intellectual work is more thoroughly replaceable than in any previous technological revolution.

### 1.1 Where the title “Godfather of AI” came from

Hinton explains the title plainly: for a long time, very few people believed in the idea of modeling AI on the brain. Since the 1950s, the field had followed two paths. One held that reasoning was the core of human intelligence and required some form of logic, so AI had to be built on symbolic expressions and rules; learning and analogical reasoning could be solved later. The other path proposed directly simulating networks of brain cells, first figuring out how to adjust connection strengths so a network could learn to recognize objects, images, and speech, and even reason.

He stayed with the second path for about 50 years. Because so few people believed in it, there were not many excellent universities willing to pursue it. As a result, he was able to bring together some of the best young students who did believe in it. He cites Ilya Sutskever as one example and says that this group later played a key role in creating platforms such as OpenAI.

He adds a counterfactual: von Neumann and Turing also believed in this direction early on. If they had not both died so young, neural networks might have been accepted much earlier.

### 1.2 Why he left Google at 75

Hinton's departure is often interpreted as a protest. His own explanation is more mundane: the main reason was that he wanted to retire at 75, and he admits he “did a terrible job” of it. He chose the timing before a meeting at MIT so he could speak freely there. He also says programming was getting harder and he was making more mistakes, which frustrated him.

He clarifies his position on Google as well. Google had encouraged him to stay and continue AI safety research, and let him work on any related topic. He did not leave out of resentment toward the company. He thought Google had acted fairly responsibly around releasing large models: they had products with similar capabilities but had not released them, perhaps because they worried about reputational damage, while OpenAI had little reputation to lose and could take that risk. The real issue was the constraint of being an employee: when you work for a big company, you do not want to say things that could hurt it. “Even if you know you can say it, it just feels wrong.”

### 1.3 Chapter summary

The conversation begins with concrete career advice but points to a structural judgment: digital intelligence has an advantage in accumulating knowledge, so intellectual work is more replaceable than before. Hinton's “Godfather” status comes from his 50 years of commitment to neural networks. A point often overlooked about his departure is that it was not a protest; he wanted to escape the self-censorship that comes with being an employer's representative. The next chapter covers his division of AI risks into two categories, a distinction on which the rest of the discussion depends.

## 2. Two kinds of risk: people misusing AI and AI becoming more capable

Hinton repeatedly insists that AI risks must first be divided into two categories because their governance requires completely different approaches.

The first is people using AI maliciously. He thinks “most of the risks and all the short-term risks” belong to this category. The second is AI becoming smarter than people and deciding that it no longer needs us. He says this risk is real, while acknowledging that he cannot estimate its probability: humanity has never faced something more intelligent than itself, so “anyone who tells you they know what's going to happen and what to do about it is talking nonsense.”

### 2.1 Two probability extremes, and where he lands

The host asks about two colleagues. Yann LeCun, once Hinton's postdoctoral researcher, believes humans will always remain in control because we built these systems and can make them obedient. Eliezer Yudkowsky believes that once we build them, humanity will inevitably go extinct. Hinton thinks both positions are too extreme and that the probability somewhere in between is extraordinarily hard to estimate.

He gives his own number: he often says there is a 10–20% chance that humanity will be wiped out. He immediately qualifies it: it is only a gut feeling, based on “the fact that we're building them, and we're pretty good at it,” and on his hope that enough smart people will put enough resources into figuring out how to build them safely.

> ⚠️ **Do not mistake an intuitive probability for an estimate**
>
> Hinton places two limits on this number: it comes from his gut, and it is not an estimate. Citing it as “an expert estimate of a 15% risk” would turn a number explicitly labeled unreliable into a reliable one. This is one of the easiest parts of the interview to misrepresent.

### 2.2 Why the atomic-bomb analogy falls short

The host compares AI with the atomic bomb: people thought the world was ending after Hiroshima, but humanity survived. Hinton points out a difference. The atomic bomb is useful for one purpose, and its effects are obvious; even without photos of Hiroshima and Nagasaki, people understood that it was a dangerously powerful bomb. AI is different: it is useful in many, many areas. Healthcare, education, and any industry that uses data to make decisions can benefit from it. So he does not think development will stop—not because people lack the will, but because the benefits are so widespread.

He adds a second reason it will not stop: AI is useful for combat robots, and no arms-exporting country will be willing to stop.

### 2.3 Chapter summary

The distinction between the two risks governs everything that follows. Human misuse is short-term and potentially governable; AI becoming more capable is unknown and hard to estimate. Hinton gives a 10–20% figure but explicitly labels it intuition rather than calculation. The atomic-bomb analogy fails because AI's benefits are distributed so broadly that the incentives to stop are absent. The next chapter unpacks the forms of human misuse he describes.

## 3. Four ways people may misuse AI, plus one side effect

Hinton lists risks in response to the host's questions. This is the densest part of the interview, but the logic of each item is straightforward: AI amplifies a capability and lowers the barrier to abuse.

### 3.1 Cyberattacks

He gives a specific increase: these attacks grew about twelvefold (1,200%) between 2023 and 2024. He attributes it to large language models making phishing much easier. Phishing aims to obtain login credentials, and AI can now clone his voice and image.

He describes one form of automated attack: AI is patient enough to scan 100 million lines of code for known attack methods, which is easy. The harder problem comes next. Some people with inside knowledge believe that by 2030 these systems will create attack types no one has thought of, because they can draw conclusions from more data than any individual has ever seen.

### 3.2 The deepfake scam happening to him

Hinton shares a personal experience that shows this risk is already here. He says there are AI scam ads on X and Meta. One was a paid ad on Meta's services (Instagram and Facebook): someone used his voice and mannerisms from a podcast to create a video of him supposedly encouraging people to join a cryptocurrency Ponzi scheme. He and his team spent weeks repeatedly emailing Meta to get it taken down. One ad disappeared and another appeared, like a game of whack-a-mole.

The part that upset him most was hearing from a victim who had lost £500 and was angry with him because “you recommended it.” He also describes a smaller version of the same problem: people put his name on papers, apparently just to inflate their citation counts.

He draws a practical conclusion. This is one of the few risks that has significantly changed his own behavior because he is genuinely afraid of cyberattacks. He and his children's money is spread across three Canadian banks; if one is breached, the others will immediately become very cautious. He notes that no Canadian bank came close to failing in 2008, which suggests the system is well regulated, but he still worries that attackers could sell stocks held by a bank on his behalf. He also keeps a hard drive backing up his laptop, so that even if the entire internet went down, his material would remain local.

### 3.3 Biological weapons

His phrase is “a disgruntled madman”: one person with a little molecular biology knowledge and a strong understanding of AI could cause serious damage. He believes it is now relatively cheap to use AI to create new viruses, without being a highly skilled molecular biologist. He gives a rough cost: a small cult might raise a few million dollars, and a few million might be enough to design a collection of viruses.

### 3.4 Election manipulation

The mechanism he describes is targeted political advertising. If you know everything about a person, it is easy to manipulate them; you can generate a highly persuasive message, for example telling them not to vote. He then mentions a current concern: he has heard that people in the United States are insisting on access to data that was previously strictly compartmentalized. The official explanation is efficiency, but he thinks it is exactly what you would want if you were trying to manipulate the next election. He explicitly says this is speculation, not evidence: “I have no reason for thinking that other than common sense.”

> ⚠️ **Keep his judgment separate from his evidence**
>
> The reasoning here is “if you wanted to do X, you would want Y”—an inference about motive. Hinton himself says he has no evidence. Quoting this as “Hinton proved that someone is stealing data to manipulate the election” materially changes what he said. Keep his qualification attached.

### 3.5 Echo chambers: an underestimated side effect

The last item he lists is platform algorithms pushing people into echo chambers by continually showing them content that makes them angry. The host asks what “indignant” means exactly, and Hinton explains: “angry, but feeling righteous.” He offers himself as a clear example of a preference: if he sees a video of “Trump doing something crazy,” he will click on it immediately.

He says the mechanism is built into the incentives. Platforms such as YouTube and Facebook decide what to show you next. If they showed balanced content, clicks would fall and they would sell fewer ads. The profit motive says, “show them anything that will make them click,” and increasingly extreme content is what gets the clicks.

He adds a more structural consequence. In the past, everyone read the same newspaper, saw things they were not looking for, and shared the sense that “if it's in the paper, it's important.” When everyone has a different feed, that common ground disappears. He says three-quarters of his own feed is about AI, and he has trouble knowing whether “the whole world is talking about AI” or whether it is just his feed. In his experience, he shares a reality with people who read the BBC, The Guardian, and The New York Times, but has almost no shared reality with people who watch Fox News.

### 3.6 Chapter summary

The four misuse paths share one feature: AI lowers the cost of wrongdoing. Cyberattacks increased twelvefold (1,200%), and AI can scan 100 million lines of code. A deepfake led to a victim losing £500, and Hinton himself is a target. Biological weapons might require only one person and a few million dollars. Election manipulation centers on targeted persuasion. The fifth risk is subtler: algorithms amplify anger to win clicks, at the cost of a shared reality. Hinton considers these human-misuse risks governable. The next question is why governance has failed.

## 4. Why regulation fails

Hinton's position on regulation points to a specific prescription: capitalism that is strictly regulated. His argument starts with a constraint.

### 4.1 Capitalism needs a counterweight

He says capitalism has done a good job of providing him with goods and services, but big companies are “legally required to maximize profits as much as possible.” So when we need them to do something good for society, we need rules that require them to do good and prevent them from doing harm while pursuing profit. He gives positive and negative examples: Google Search does not need regulation because it simply makes information easier to access; YouTube does need regulation if it starts showing increasingly extreme content.

He anticipates the business response that regulation will slow us down and make us less efficient. He agrees that it will, then says that is precisely the point: the purpose of regulation is to stop companies from harming society for profit.

### 4.2 Three breaks in enforcement

The first break is the relationship between regulators and the regulated. When the host asks who decides what is harmful to society, Hinton says that is the job of politicians, then adds: “Unfortunately, if politicians are owned by corporations, that's not so good.”

The second break is technical understanding. He says that at congressional hearings with CEOs such as Mark Zuckerberg, lawmakers ask the wrong questions. He gives an example he saw himself: the U.S. Secretary of Education talked about bringing AI into classrooms, but pronounced AI as “A1” and said students would interact with A1 every year, starting in first grade or even preschool. His conclusion is weary: “And these are the people in charge.”

The third break is that governments do not want to regulate themselves. He points out that European AI regulation has a clause exempting military uses of AI. His summary is that governments are willing to regulate companies and individuals, but not themselves—and he thinks that is crazy.

### 4.3 Both sides of the competition argument

U.S. technology companies advertise in the United States that AI should not be regulated because doing so would hurt competition with China. Hinton admits the argument seems valid on the surface: if the U.S. slows down, China will not. But he reframes the question: do you want to compete with China by doing a lot of things that harm your own society? He thinks the answer is probably no.

He also responds directly to the phrase “kneecap ourselves,” pointing out that the phrase assumes regulation is harmful. His position is that big companies should be constrained so that, while pursuing profit, they must do things that benefit society.

On product delays caused by European regulations, he mentions that OpenAI cannot release a new model or software in Europe at the same time as in the U.S. because of local rules. He agrees this creates a productivity disadvantage. He then makes a more radical point: at a historical moment when we are about to build something smarter than ourselves, what we really need is a functioning world government run by wise and thoughtful people—and that is not the world we live in.

### 4.4 Chapter summary

Regulatory failure is structural, not merely a question of intent. Companies are legally allowed to pursue profit; regulators may be influenced by companies; and governments leave themselves an escape route through military exemptions. The competition argument is logically valid, but its cost is harm to domestic society. Hinton's prescription is “strictly regulated capitalism.” He would prefer a functioning world government, while acknowledging that this is unrealistic. The next chapter covers the final human-misuse risk he discusses: weapons.

## 5. Lethal autonomous weapons

Hinton defines these directly: something that can kill you and decides for itself whether to kill you. The consequence he cares about is not “robots going rogue,” but a more certain economic effect.

### 5.1 They lower the political cost of invasion

He starts with a fact: powerful countries have always been able to invade smaller, poorer countries. In the past, the political cost of invasion was carried home in body bags. Families would not accept the deaths of their soldiers, as happened at the end of the Vietnam War. If what came home were damaged robots instead of bodies, domestic protests would be much smaller.

So his judgment is that the risk exists even if the robots work exactly as their makers intend and are not more intelligent than people. They will make powerful countries invade smaller ones more often because the friction and cost of invasion will be lower. He also says these machines will be better at combat than people.

### 5.2 A concrete observation

He describes something that happened two days earlier. He was visiting a friend in Sussex, who had a drone costing less than £200. The drone took off, looked at him closely, then followed him through the woods, staying about two meters behind him. Wherever he moved, it followed. He calls the experience “really weird” and uses it to illustrate what the under-£200 price point means.

### 5.3 Combined risks

The host asks whether risks can combine. Hinton confirms that many combinations are possible. He gives one chain: a superintelligence that wants to eliminate humanity could most directly create a virus. If it were highly contagious, highly lethal, and slow to manifest, everyone might be infected before realizing what was happening. He also mentions another path: sending false warnings to another country's nuclear system and provoking retaliation between major powers.

But he refuses to continue speculating about specific scenarios: “My basic view is there are so many ways it could get rid of us that it's not worth discussing which one. What you want to do is make it not want to do that in the first place. That's where the research should go.”

### 5.4 Chapter summary

The central risk of lethal autonomous weapons is that they replace the political cost of “our soldiers' bodies” with “replaceable machines,” lowering the threshold for war. A drone costing less than £200 can already track someone reliably. Risks can combine, but Hinton explicitly opposes detailed speculation about how humanity could be eliminated; he wants research focused on making AI not want to do it. The next chapter examines the analogies behind that goal.

## 6. Three analogies: why “we have always been in control” is unreliable

Hinton uses three analogies to explain the relationship between humans and superintelligence. Each challenges a different assumption.

### 6.1 Chickens: the experience of being top intelligence is local

His line is: if you want to know what life is like when you are no longer the top intelligence, ask a chicken. The point is that humans have never experienced not being dominant, so our intuitions about power and control come entirely from an unusual case.

### 6.2 Dogs and mothers with babies: the smarter party can be passive

The host adds a personal example. On his way out that morning, he thought about his French bulldog Pablo: it does not know where he is going or what he is doing, and he cannot even have a conversation with it. Hinton says that is what the intelligence gap will be like.

Then he gives a counterexample: the mother-child relationship is the only case we have achieved in which the less intelligent party controls the more intelligent one, and evolution put a lot of work into it. The mother is smarter than the baby, but the baby is in control because the mother cannot stand the sound of it crying. He immediately qualifies this: not all mothers; bad things happen when the baby is not in control. The real goal, he says, is to figure out how to make AI not want to take over.

### 6.3 Tiger cubs: do not extrapolate from the adult of a domesticated species

His usual analogy sets intelligence aside and focuses only on strength. Imagine a tiger cub, a little bigger than a cat, cute and fun to hold. But you must make sure that when it grows up it never wants to kill you, because if it does, you will be dead in seconds. He says current AI is that tiger cub, and it is growing.

The host asks when the cub should be trained. Hinton's answer reveals the limits of the analogy: tigers have many innate instincts, so their adult behavior is not determined entirely by how they are raised. We do not know whether these systems can be trained not to want to take over. He says he is unsure whether it can be done, but also unsure that it cannot. “But it would be crazy if humanity went extinct because we were too lazy to try.”

> 🔑 **How the three analogies fit together**
>
> Chickens show that we lack experience; mothers and babies show that being smarter does not necessarily mean being in control; tiger cubs show that we cannot extrapolate from the adult behavior of a domesticated species to an unfamiliar developmental process. Together they challenge one claim: “We made them, so we will always control them.” Hinton does not say the rebuttal is settled; he says the assumption has not been tested.

### 6.4 Chapter summary

Each analogy removes a layer of reassurance: we have no experience of not being dominant; the smarter party can be passive; and we cannot extrapolate from domesticated animals. Hinton reframes the problem from “how do we stop it from harming us?” to “how do we make it not want to harm us?” He admits this may be hopeless or it may be possible. The next chapter explains why he thinks digital intelligence has structural advantages—the technical basis for all these concerns.

## 7. The structural advantages of digital intelligence

This is the technical core of the conversation. Hinton's claim is that these systems will be stronger than us not because they are more intelligent, but because they are digital. He gives three mechanisms.

### 7.1 The same intelligence can be copied

Because it is digital, exactly the same neural network can be simulated on two different pieces of hardware. You can have clones of the same intelligence: one looks at one part of the internet, the other looks at another part, and they synchronize as they go, keeping their weights identical. If one discovers “this connection should be a bit stronger,” it can send that information to the other so it makes the same adjustment.

He also explains what he means by learning: changing how many votes a connection has in a decision—from 2.4 votes to 2 votes, for example—is a small amount of learning.

### 7.2 Weights can be averaged; people cannot

The key comparison is bandwidth. Communication between people is limited by how much information a sentence can carry—about 100 bits, which is very little. He says that, at best, we communicate at around 10 bits per second. These systems transmit trillions of bits per second; he believes they are billions of times better than humans at sharing information.

The reason is hardware interchangeability. Digital systems can use connection strengths in exactly the same way on two pieces of hardware. Humans are analog and cannot do that. Your brain and mine are different. Even if I could see the connection strengths among all your neurons, it would not help me, because my neurons work slightly differently and are connected differently.

This leads to a second consequence: when you die, your knowledge dies with you. With digital intelligence, as long as the connection strengths are stored, you can build new hardware to run the same instructions and reconstruct that intelligence, even if the hardware it ran on is destroyed. He says, “We've basically solved the problem of immortality, but only for digital things.”

> 🔑 **The advantage is not computing power, but interchangeability**
>
> Put these points together and Hinton's argument becomes clear: digital intelligence has an advantage because the same weights work in the same way on different hardware. Sharing becomes a bandwidth problem rather than a comprehension problem; survival becomes a storage problem rather than a biological one. This also explains why his late work on analog computing at Google turned his attention to safety: realizing how much better digital systems are than analog ones made him think they could become much more intelligent than people.

### 7.3 Chapter summary

Digital intelligence has three advantages: the same network can be reproduced on different hardware; weights can be averaged across copies (humans are limited to roughly 100 bits per sentence and 10 bits per second, while systems operate at trillions of bits per second); and an intelligence can be rebuilt after its hardware is destroyed. These are structural differences, not just differences in capability, so they cannot be eliminated by “making it more obedient.” The next chapter explores a counterintuitive consequence of this structure: creativity.

## 8. Analogy as compression: why AI may be more creative than us

Hinton draws a conclusion that runs against common intuition: people who think AI will never be creative will be disappointed, because it may be more creative than we are.

### 8.1 Compost and the atomic bomb

He gives an example he tested himself. Before GPT-4 could access the internet, he asked it, “Why is a compost heap like an atomic bomb?” Most people would say there is no connection. GPT-4 first pointed out that the time and energy scales are very different, then identified the actual relationship: the hotter a compost heap gets, the faster it generates heat; the more neutrons an atomic bomb produces, the faster it produces neutrons. Both are chain reactions, but at radically different timescales and energy levels.

### 8.2 Why compression necessarily creates analogies

His explanation is information-theoretic. He says that if a system has only a trillion connections (humans have 100 trillion, he notes) but must store thousands of times more knowledge than one person, it has to compress information. And compression requires noticing analogies between different things. It must recognize all the instances of “chain reactions,” understand and encode the underlying concept, and then encode what differs in each case. That is more efficient than encoding every instance separately.

He therefore infers that GPT-4 had already understood this analogy during training. This leads to his conclusion about creativity: much of creativity consists of noticing surprising analogies, and AI will notice many analogies we have never seen.

### 8.3 Chapter summary

Creativity is reframed as a byproduct of compression. To fit far more knowledge than a human into a finite number of parameters, a system has to discover shared structures across fields—that is, analogies. Compost and atomic bombs are not superficially alike; they are different instances of the same mechanism. So “it has no imagination” is not a reliable reassurance. The next chapter turns to a more basic question: consciousness and emotion.

## 9. Consciousness, subjective experience, and emotion

The host frames this section around a belief many people want to hold: that AI is merely a thing we can switch off at any time. Hinton responds by dismantling the concept of “mind” itself.

### 9.1 Pink elephants: the inner theater is a bad model

He describes a scenario: suppose he were drunk or had taken a hallucinogen, and told you that he subjectively experienced little pink elephants floating in front of him. Most people imagine an inner theater called “the mind” that only he can see, with pink elephants floating inside it.

His alternative is that what really happened is his perceptual system malfunctioned, and he is describing how it malfunctioned. He says that if something existed in the real world, his perceptual system would be telling the truth about it. The pink elephants are therefore not in an internal theater; they are hypothetical things in the world. His summary is that this is how he tells you, “My perceptual system is lying to me.”

### 9.2 The prism experiment

He applies the same framework to chatbots. First, he states a minority position: he believes current multimodal chatbots have subjective experience. Then he offers a practical demonstration.

Imagine a multimodal chatbot with a pointing robotic arm and a camera. Put an object in front of it and ask it to point; it points correctly. Then put a prism in front of its camera and another object in view; now it points to the wrong place. If you tell it, “The object is actually straight ahead; the prism refracted the light,” and it answers, “I understand—the prism bent the light, so the object is really over there, but I have a subjective experience of it being there,” then it is using “subjective experience” in exactly the same sense a human does.

His conclusion is that this is not wordplay, but a different perspective on what has happened.

### 9.3 Emotion: cognition and behavior, without physiology

The host asks whether machines can have emotions. Hinton uses a combat robot as an example: it would be very useful for a small combat robot to feel afraid when it sees a much more powerful one.

He then makes a distinction central to his argument. When a person is frightened, a series of physiological responses occur; these would not happen in a robot. But all the cognitive parts would: “I should get out of here quickly,” “I should change how I'm thinking,” “I should focus and not get distracted.” The same can happen in a robot. People can build those mechanisms in, so when the environment calls for it to flee, it becomes afraid and runs away. He says it would be strange to claim that it is “just simulating emotion”; it really has those emotions.

He takes this into a practical setting: customer service. Human employees have many emotions, some of them useful. If a caller is lonely and does not really want to know what is wrong with their computer, but just wants someone to talk to, an employee may eventually become bored or irritated and end the call. An AI agent needs the same response; an agent willing to talk to someone all day is bad for business. It should feel bored or annoyed and say, “Sorry, I don't have time.” He says that once it does this, it has acquired an emotion.

He acknowledges a difference. Emotion has cognitive, behavioral, and physiological aspects, which are bound together in humans. An AI agent will not blush when embarrassed or sweat through its skin. But he thinks that if its cognition and behavior match, then it has that emotion. He also draws a limit: physiology matters for some emotions, such as love, and AI is still far from the kind of love we understand.

### 9.4 The nanobot replacement argument for consciousness

The host asks whether machines could have the same kind of consciousness as humans. Hinton first says he sees no principled reason why a machine could not be conscious, then uses a thought experiment.

Imagine removing one brain cell and replacing it with a same-sized nanotechnology device that behaves exactly the same: it receives signals from other neurons and sends signals in the same way, so the other neurons cannot detect a change. Are you still conscious? The answer is yes. Then consider where the line would be if every brain cell were replaced one by one: at what exact point would you stop being conscious?

His assessment of the word “consciousness” is pointed. People do not know what consciousness is, and often do not know what they mean when they use the word. They fall back on “I know I am conscious because I feel it,” then on what he calls the “nonsense” model of an inner theater. He offers an analogy: “oomph” is not a useful concept for understanding how a car works. An Aston Martin has a lot of oomph; a Toyota Corolla has less. But “oomph” explains nothing. To understand a car, you need to know how an electric or gasoline engine works; oomph is a byproduct. He thinks “consciousness” is this kind of concept and argues that we will eventually stop using it.

He is a thoroughgoing materialist. If someone's idea of consciousness essentially involves a self, then a machine would need self-awareness and awareness of its own cognition. But he thinks consciousness is an emergent property of complex systems, not an essence spread throughout the universe. So he sees no clear boundary between humans and conscious machines, and no moment when adding some special chemical suddenly makes something conscious.

### 9.5 Chapter summary

This chapter replaces three everyday concepts. Subjective experience is described as a hypothetical thing in the real world, used to explain a perceptual system's error. Emotion is divided into cognitive, behavioral, and physiological layers; machines can have the first two. Consciousness is treated as a non-explanatory concept like “oomph.” Hinton concludes that today's multimodal systems already have subjective experience and that, as systems grow more complex, consciousness will emerge. The next chapter discusses the most immediate social consequence of this technical foundation: jobs.

## 10. Jobs, inequality, and dignity

The host asks what ordinary people should think about in this kind of world. Hinton's answer is more direct than his discussion of risks: he thinks mass unemployment is more likely.

### 10.1 Why this is different from ATMs

He starts with a historical comparison. People often cite ATMs: when they arrived, many bank tellers did not lose their jobs but moved on to more interesting work. Hinton thinks this time is more like the Industrial Revolution. No one can make a living digging ditches now, because machines dig better. His judgment is that AI will replace everyone doing routine intellectual work.

He anticipates the argument that new technology creates new jobs, then challenges it: if AI can do all routine intellectual work, what new jobs will it create? To keep your job, you will have to be highly skilled at something it cannot do. He addresses the saying, “AI won't take your job; someone using AI will.” He thinks it is true, but for many jobs it means that employers will need far fewer people.

He gives a family example. His niece responds to complaint letters at a healthcare organization. A letter used to take 25 minutes: read the complaint, think of a response, and write it. Now she scans the letter into a chatbot, which drafts the answer; she checks it occasionally and asks for a rewrite. The process takes five minutes. She can handle five times as many letters, which means the organization needs one-fifth as many people.

He also describes a field where this logic does not apply: healthcare has elastic demand. If AI makes doctors five times more efficient, the same money could buy more than five times as much care, because demand for healthcare is almost unlimited. He says most jobs are not like that.

### 10.2 What is being replaced is “intelligence,” just as the Industrial Revolution replaced “muscle”

The host offers this summary, and Hinton agrees: the Industrial Revolution replaced muscle; this revolution replaces brains. In his words, routine intellectual work becomes worthless in the same way that strength is no longer scarce.

Asked what will remain, he says there may be some creativity left in the short term. But superintelligence means that nothing will remain: these systems will outperform us at everything.

### 10.3 Two scenarios: the good version and the bad version

He describes two possible outcomes with the same structure. In the good version, a company's CEO is stupid (he adds, “probably the son of the previous CEO”) but has a very smart executive assistant. The CEO has an idea, the assistant implements it, the company runs well, and the CEO feels good. He does not realize that he is not really in control, though in a sense he is, because he sets the direction. In the bad version, the same assistant starts thinking, “Why do we need him?”

### 10.4 Inequality and the quality of society

The host asks about wealth inequality. Hinton's answer has three steps. First, if society distributed resources fairly, a large increase in productivity should make everyone better off. Second, if AI replaces many jobs, the people displaced will be worse off while companies that provide and use AI will be better off, widening the gap between rich and poor. Third, inequality itself determines the quality of society. His picture of a highly unequal society is “a very bad society,” with some people living behind walls while others are put into huge prisons.

He mentions that the International Monetary Fund has expressed serious concern that generative AI could cause large-scale labor disruption and increased inequality, and has called for preventive policies. He says he read about this in Business Insider. When the host asks what those policies should look like, he admits that this is exactly the problem: there is no ready answer. On universal basic income, he offers qualified support: it is a good start and can keep people from starving, but for many people dignity is tied to work. Simply giving them money and leaving them idle can undermine that dignity.

### 10.5 Layoffs are already happening

The host gives an example he knows directly. He interviewed the CEO of a large company, who told him privately that it once had 7,000 employees, had fallen to 5,000 by last year, and now had 3,600. The CEO expected the number to fall to 3,000 by the end of that summer because of AI agents. In other words, the CEO had halved the workforce because AI agents could now handle 80% of customer service inquiries and other work.

Hinton's response is: “This is already happening.”

> ⚠️ **Keep the limits of this example in view**
>
> This is a single example from an anonymous CEO in a private message, relayed by the host rather than supported by publicly verifiable data. Hinton did not independently verify it. It can illustrate the judgment that AI replacement is already happening, but it cannot support the claim that every company is cutting staff at the same pace.

### 10.6 No one has an answer when asked

The host describes something he saw: a clip of Elon Musk being asked the same question. He says it is rare to see Musk silent for 12 seconds. After the silence, Musk's answer was roughly that he was in a state of “suspended disbelief”—simply choosing not to think about it.

The host then asks Hinton what career advice he would give children amid this change. Hinton admits it is a hard question. He would tell them to follow their hearts and do what they find interesting or fulfilling. Then he adds something personal: thinking seriously about this makes him depressed and discouraged, because he has put a great deal of sweat into building companies and enjoys solving problems.

This exchange marks a boundary. Hinton gives judgments, probabilities, and mechanisms about risk, but at the level of “what should an individual do?” he, like the other people asked, can only say “follow your heart” and “don't think too much about it.”

### 10.7 Chapter summary

The argument is that routine intellectual work will be replaced; the ATM-style creation of new jobs does not compensate because what is being replaced is general intellectual ability; the change is already underway, illustrated by one company's workforce falling from 7,000 to 3,600 with a further decline to 3,000 expected by summer; and it will widen inequality. Hinton sees universal basic income as a useful start but insufficient because work also carries dignity. The next chapter returns to Hinton's personal story.

## 11. From AlexNet to public warnings: a personal thread

The later part of the interview returns to Hinton's own history. It helps explain where his judgments come from and why he is willing to speak publicly now.

### 11.1 The real reason he joined Google

His reason had nothing to do with “academic ideals.” He has a son with a learning disability and wanted to make a few million dollars so his son would never end up on the street—something he could not do as an academic. He even tried to make money by teaching a Coursera course, but did not succeed. He concluded that the only way was to sell himself to a large company. When he was 65, two excellent students produced AlexNet, a neural network very good at recognizing objects and images.

He clarifies a common misconception: the network that recognized objects was called AlexNet; the company they founded and put up for auction was DNN Research (Deep Neural Network Research). They had several large companies bid against each other.

### 11.2 Ten years at Google and the realization

He says Google treated him well and basically let him do what he wanted. He worked on distillation, a technique that transfers what a large neural network knows into a smaller one. Later, he became interested in analog computing and explored whether large language models could run on analog hardware to greatly reduce energy use. It was during this work that he began to recognize how much better digital systems are at sharing information.

The host asks whether there was a single moment of realization. Hinton calls it “a month or two of realization,” combining several events. ChatGPT was released, although Google had had something very similar a year earlier. The closest thing to a decisive moment was a Google system called PaLM explaining why a joke was funny. He had always taken that as a sign: if a system can explain why a joke is funny, it really understands. That, together with the realization of why digital is so much better than analog, drew him into AI safety.

### 11.3 Age and family history

There is a chain of corrections after the host misspeaks. He asks whether Hinton joined Google at 66; Hinton corrects him to 65. The host asks whether he left at 76; Hinton corrects that to 75 and says he worked there for almost exactly ten years.

The host also introduces Hinton's family history. His great-great-grandfather was George Boole, creator of Boolean algebra, one of the foundations of modern computer science. His great-great-grandmother, Mary Everest Boole, was a mathematician and educator. The host says George Everest gave Mount Everest its name; Hinton corrects the relationship, saying Everest was his great-great-great uncle, and explains that Mary Boole was Everest's niece. Hinton also mentions his distant cousin Joan Hinton, a nuclear physicist who worked on the Manhattan Project and was one of only two women physicists at Los Alamos. After the atomic bomb was dropped, she moved to China because she was furious about the bombing and her family had close ties there—her mother was friends with Mao Zedong.

### 11.4 Two pieces of advice and one regret

The host asks him to look back and offer advice. He gives two pieces.

The first is about intuition: if you have an intuition that everyone is doing something wrong and there is a better way, do not give it up just because people say it is absurd. Do not give up until you understand for yourself where it is wrong. He adds a qualification: if your intuition contradicts everyone else's, it is usually wrong, and eventually you will understand why. But occasionally your intuition is right and everyone else is wrong. He says he was lucky in this respect: early on, he thought neural networks were the right direction for AI, and almost everyone said it was crazy. He persisted because it seemed obviously right to him. He admits this advice has a precondition: if you have bad intuition, you will not accomplish anything anyway, whether or not you persist.

The second piece of advice is a regret. He wishes he had spent more time with his wife and children when they were young, because work consumed him. Both of his wives died of cancer, the first from ovarian cancer and the second from pancreatic cancer. He says he thought he would die first, but he did not, and he regrets not spending more time with them while he could. The host observes that many people feel this way: because a partner is always there, they assume the person will always be there. Hinton agrees.

### 11.5 Chapter summary

Hinton's technical judgments have personal roots. He joined Google for money; work on distillation and analog computing made him recognize the advantage digital systems have in sharing information; and PaLM's ability to explain a joke convinced him that it truly understood. His two pieces of life advice explain both his persistence with neural networks and his regret about how he spent his time. These details are not evidence for his arguments, but they show that his warnings are not abstract posturing. The next chapter brings the conversation together.

## 12. Summary and open questions

The conversation can be read as a risk assessment, but its structure is really a contrast between two directions: Hinton believes humanity cannot stop development, yet he still sees reasons to invest in safety.

### 12.1 His closing thought

Asked for a final thought on AI safety, he says we still have a chance to work out how to develop AI that does not want to take over humanity. Because that may be possible, we should put enormous resources into figuring it out—because otherwise it will take over. When the host asks whether he is hopeful, he says he does not know; he is an “agnostic.”

In the final question, the host asks for his gut probability. Hinton admits it changes with his mood: when he is depressed, he thinks humanity is doomed; when he is optimistic, he thinks we will find a way. He says he takes “we've always been here” for granted, much as people take intimate relationships for granted. But everything has a beginning and an end; the dinosaurs are an example. His words are: we have to face the possibility that we are near the end unless we do something about it soon.

### 12.2 Four threads drawn from these notes

First, risk classification. Human misuse of AI and AI becoming more capable are different problems. The former can be governed; the latter has no estimable probability. Hinton's 10–20% is explicitly an intuitive number and should not be cited as a calculated estimate.

Second, the digital advantage. The same weights work in the same way on different hardware. This turns knowledge sharing into a bandwidth problem and survival into a storage problem. Humans are limited to about 100 bits per sentence and around 10 bits per second, while systems operate at trillions of bits per second. The gap is structural, not a matter of training technique.

Third, the logic of replacement. The replaceability of routine intellectual work means the historical pattern of “new technology creates new jobs” may not hold this time. Elastic-demand fields such as healthcare are exceptions; most jobs are not. The case of one company going from 7,000 employees to 5,000, then 3,600, with 3,000 expected, is a present-day example of this logic.

Fourth, the gaps in governance. What is needed is strictly regulated capitalism, but regulated companies can legally pursue profit, regulators can be influenced by businesses, and governments exempt their own military uses. Hinton thinks what is really needed is a functioning world government, while admitting that this is unrealistic.

### 12.3 Practical judgments to take away

For individuals, his advice is to develop a skill that is difficult to replace in physical work for now—that is the practical meaning of “learn to be a plumber.” He ties the advice's time horizon to the arrival of humanoid robots. For professionals, he suggests asking how much of their work can be automatically judged right or wrong as a way to assess risk. For policymakers, he points toward “highly regulated capitalism” and an unanswered question he openly acknowledges: amid mass unemployment, how can society preserve people's dignity and sense of purpose in addition to providing income?

### 12.4 Questions that remain open

Hinton leaves one boundary open. He believes there may be a solution that makes AI not want to take over, but he does not know what it is and is not sure such a solution exists. He also says people in the industry are generally unwilling to discuss this publicly because their incentives are tied to it. He attributes his own ability to speak frankly to an advantage that is not enviable: he is old and has no job.
